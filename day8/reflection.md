# Course Reflection: Web Foundations, API Engineering & System Design

The most difficult concept in this course was mastering concurrency control and race condition prevention in distributed systems, particularly understanding why standard database queries fail during high-traffic flash sales. Early on, I conceptualized transactions as simple checks followed by writes, which functioned in isolated testing but introduced critical double-booking vulnerabilities under simultaneous requests. I overcame this by studying database isolation levels, experimenting with PostgreSQL row-level locks, and implementing atomic conditional updates (`UPDATE ... WHERE status = 'AVAILABLE'`) combined with Redis distributed locks. Seeing how a single atomic statement eliminates race conditions without creating deadlocks was an eye-opening breakthrough.

Based on the feedback I received on my capstone design for QuickNotes and TicketHub, the primary area I would improve is the failure recovery and resilience testing of the queuing architecture. While my current design includes a virtual waiting room and RabbitMQ for asynchronous tasks, I would like to incorporate dead-letter queues, exponential backoff with jitter for payment retries, and comprehensive circuit breakers. Additionally, adding real-time seat status updates via WebSocket channels rather than short-polling would significantly improve user responsiveness during high contention.

Next, I plan to dive deeper into distributed consensus algorithms (such as Raft and Paxos) and cloud-native Kubernetes orchestration. Understanding how partitioned databases manage consistency versus availability under network partitions will allow me to design enterprise-grade, multi-region distributed systems capable of scaling to tens of millions of users.
